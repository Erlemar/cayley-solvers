# New Research Directions for the Kaggle Megaminx shortest-path competition

## What seems most promising

The strongest opportunities are not in making the scalar value model slightly better. They are in making training match the search procedure you actually run, then exploiting the search traces you already generate at scale. The literature points most strongly toward five families of ideas: beam-search-aware training objectives that optimise beam survival rather than pure distance regression; beam-native policy learning from nested-search labels; self-improving search-to-learn loops on the hardest cases rather than generic RL from scratch; structured diversity over policy/frontier space rather than ad hoc diversity; and path-level improvement methods such as destroy–repair and elite-set relinking that operate on complete candidate solutions rather than on single states. Across modern planning, theorem proving, routing, and neural combinatorial optimisation, those mechanisms repeatedly beat “better standalone model” approaches when search is already strong and compute is substantial. citeturn18view1turn8view0turn8view1turn27view1turn25search1turn22view0turn22view1

My bottom-line view is that the best bet for getting from roughly 75.2k to below 70k is a **stack shift** rather than a **model shift**: train for beam retention and search usefulness, use giant-beam traces to generate hard-case supervision and preferences, and add a second stage that improves whole solutions by graph stitching, relinking, or destroy–repair. That matches both the public Megaminx signals and the broader literature. The public competition setup sums verified move counts over 1001 test states, and community reports on Megaminx already suggest that all-neighbour action/value prediction plus very large beams is where gains have come from, not from handcrafted Megaminx-specific tricks. citeturn14search0turn13search3turn15view0turn15view1

## Problem restated and core bottlenecks

In plain terms, this is not “solve a single puzzle well”; it is “spend a finite inference budget to shave a few moves off a thousand already-hard shortest-path problems in a 120-element permutation state space.” The official competition page describes a test set of 1001 states and scores submissions by total solution length. Public discussion also confirms that the competition uses quarter-turn metric, which makes each marginal move reduction economically meaningful at leaderboard scale. citeturn14search0turn13search3turn15view0

Given your current stack, the bottleneck is no longer generic state evaluation. The public Megaminx/CayleyPy evidence is consistent with that: strong results have come from models that predict distances for all neighbours at once and from extremely large beams, with community-reported runs using beam widths as large as \(2^{27}\) on A100 hardware for high-quality Megaminx superflip solutions. That is a direct clue that **frontier economics** matters more than another round of encoder tinkering. citeturn15view1turn13search13turn28search0

So the problem decomposes into four core bottlenecks. First, **training–inference mismatch**: a model can predict distance reasonably well and still be poor at keeping the right prefixes alive under aggressive beam pruning. Second, **long-tail hard instances**: the remaining gap is likely concentrated in a minority of puzzles where the current search-control policy makes repeated near-miss decisions. Third, **diversity quality**: once basic diversity tricks are in place, the question becomes whether the beam is diverse in *useful* ways, not merely different. Fourth, **solution-level optimisation**: after search finds a valid path, there is often latent slack that statewise models do not expose, but path-level methods can. citeturn18view1turn8view0turn8view1turn6view0turn22view1turn22view0

## What the iterative searches changed my mind about

The first search round, in the obvious neighbourhood of Rubik/Megaminx solving and neural-guided search, pushed me away from “find a better value target” and toward “train the model for the beam you actually run.” BEAR is from recommendation rather than puzzles, but its diagnosis is extremely relevant: high global probability does not guarantee survival under beam pruning, and optimising a necessary condition for beam survival can recover most of the gain of full beam-simulation training without the cost. In parallel, the beam-policy literature and policy-based learning beam search make the same point in a more classical form: if inference uses beam search, training should generate labels at the level of beam decisions, not isolated state values. Q* search further strengthens the action-centric view by showing that state–action estimates can yield much more efficient search control than value-only expansion logic. citeturn18view1turn8view0turn8view1turn5view0

The second round, looking at theorem proving, offline RL, and self-improvement, made generic RL look less attractive and **deterministic, search-derived supervision** look much more attractive. BFS-Prover succeeds not through vague RL, but through expert iteration on hard cases, filtering out easy examples and improving the policy with preference-style updates from verified search outcomes. LeanProgress trains on search-generated proof trees to predict remaining steps, and the 2026 planning paper from Aachen explicitly uses a self-improving loop in which search guided by learned Q-values creates the next round of training data. Search on the Replay Buffer adds another useful abstraction: when long-horizon planning is hard, build a graph over previously seen states and learn which bridges are real rather than forcing the model to discover everything online. citeturn19view0turn19view1turn27view1turn27view0

The third round, deliberately outside the immediate puzzle literature, changed the post-processing view. Large-neighbourhood search, local rewriting, path relinking, solver portfolios, and even SAT restart work all converge on the same lesson: when the primitive search is already good, substantial gains often come from **search control**, **elite-memory reuse**, and **neighbourhood operators over complete candidates**. Neural LNS and dynamic partial removal show that destroy–repair can beat handcrafted local search. Path relinking literature shows that improvements often come from elite-solution trajectories, especially near the ends of the path, where truncated relinking is efficient. Solver-selection and multi-queue work suggests that once you already have several strong variants, “which search mode should get this budget now?” can be more important than inventing yet another single mode. citeturn22view0turn31search10turn31search8turn23search0turn22view1turn22view2turn21view1turn21view0turn20search13

## Research directions

**Beam-retention training instead of plain distance regression**  
**Core mechanism:** train the action model to satisfy a beam-specific survival condition: the actions that lie on strong search suffixes should remain inside the top-\(B\) shortlist at each depth, even if they are not globally best by scalar distance. A practical surrogate is a BEAR-style margin against the current beam cutoff, combined with ranking losses over sibling actions. **Why it might beat the current AZ/value+beam setup:** it attacks the exact failure mode of beam search: premature pruning of paths whose prefixes are locally under-ranked. Your own hypothesis about “beam-search usefulness” is right, but the literature suggests it should be formalised as **survival under pruning**, not as a second generic scalar head. **External evidence:** BEAR shows that optimising a necessary condition for beam survival captures most of the benefit of full beam-aware training with far less compute; beam-policy imitation and policy-based learning beam search both argue that beam decisions, not standalone likelihood/value targets, should be the training object. **What would make it fail:** if your current Q model already preserves the good actions at almost all depths, then this reduces to relabelling the same behaviour. **Cheap validation gate:** on archived giant-beam traces, measure the fraction of “eventually good” actions that survive top-\(k\) under the current model versus a beam-retention surrogate; if survival barely changes, stop. **Timing:** near-term. citeturn18view1turn8view0turn8view1

**Beam-native policy imitation from nested-search teachers**  
**Core mechanism:** stop asking the model to estimate cost-to-go per state, and instead ask it to rank candidate expansions the way a stronger beam would. Labels come from nested beam searches or search traces: for each frontier set, which children lead to the best downstream beam outcomes? **Why it might beat the current setup:** your strongest public signals are already on the inference side, especially large-beam traces and action shortlisting. This direction turns that into the primary supervised target. It is closer to “learn to be a beam controller” than “learn a heuristic”. **External evidence:** Negrinho et al. formulate beam search itself as the object of imitation learning and prove no-regret guarantees; P-LBS goes further by generating beam-aware ranking labels with nested beam search and bootstrapping. **What would make it fail:** if the teacher beam is too close to the student, the labels add little information; if the teacher is too expensive, label generation dominates the project. **Cheap validation gate:** take a fixed archive of frontier snapshots from hard puzzles and compare a beam-native ranker against the current Q shortlister on one-step rescue rate and two-step downstream beam quality. **Timing:** near-term. citeturn8view0turn8view1

**Hard-case self-improvement with preference-style updates**  
**Core mechanism:** use search outcomes to create pairwise or listwise preferences over actions, states, or short prefixes, then fine-tune on the hardest portion of the training distribution only. The state is the permutation plus depth/frontier metadata; the action is one of 24 generators; the reward signal is not terminal sparse reward but a deterministic preference such as “prefix A reached a shorter verified completion than prefix B under equal search budget.” **Why it might beat the current setup:** it cuts variance dramatically compared with plain RL, while still allowing policy improvement beyond the current model. It also concentrates compute where you still have headroom: hard instances and hard states. **External evidence:** BFS-Prover improves policy through expert-iteration-style loops with hard-case filtering and preference-style optimisation from verified search feedback; generalized search for planning uses search-guided Q-learning in a self-reinforcing loop; preference optimisation for combinatorial problems reports better sample efficiency than reward-scale-sensitive RL; self-improvement work in NCO shows that the model can bootstrap by sampling multiple solutions and learning from the better ones. **What would make it fail:** if the search-derived preferences are too noisy or too correlated with current model scores, the update will simply sharpen existing biases. **Cheap validation gate:** run a short fine-tune on only the hardest decile of archived states and measure whether the new policy improves best-of-\(n\) search outcomes on held-out hard puzzles without any beam-width increase. **Timing:** near-term to medium-term. citeturn19view0turn27view1turn30view0turn17view1turn30view1

**Frontier Q-search rather than value-guided beam only**  
**Core mechanism:** use state–action costs as first-class search items, closer to Q* than to classic value-guided beam. Keep node–action tuples in the open set, lazily expand only the chosen child, and let action-level estimates drive partial expansion and queue order. **Why it might beat the current setup:** this is not the same as Q shortlisting. In Q* the search algorithm itself is reorganised around action values, which can reduce waste from generating and evaluating children that will never matter. It also gives a cleaner interface for asymmetric costs, inverse actions, and bounded-suboptimal variants. **External evidence:** Q* search reports large reductions in generated nodes and heuristic evaluations, and is specifically motivated by the inefficiency of learned heuristics that evaluate full child sets in A*-like search. **What would make it fail:** Megaminx has only 24 actions, so the classical “huge action space” reason for Q* is weaker here than in some domains; the gain has to come from queue discipline and partial expansion, not from raw branching-factor reduction alone. **Cheap validation gate:** replace one beam-search endpoint with a tuple-based Q* or BWQS-style frontier policy on the same model and compare solved move counts per unit wall-clock on a hard validation slice. **Timing:** near-term. citeturn5view0

**Replay-graph stitching over archived traces**  
**Core mechanism:** turn your giant-beam traces into a directed graph of useful states, partial paths, and endpoints; learn edge costs or bridgeability; then solve hard positions by routing through archived relay states instead of relying on one continuous online search. This is effectively a “search over remembered experiences” layer on top of the current solver. **Why it might beat the current setup:** it reuses the enormous amount of search already paid for by your TPU/GPU infrastructure, and it is naturally suited to tail/path rescue and relinking. Importantly, it introduces long-horizon structure that a local action scorer may never recover. **External evidence:** Search on the Replay Buffer uses a learned graph over past observations to create subgoal chains in sparse long-horizon tasks; LeanProgress learns remaining-step prediction from search-generated proof trees; theorem-proving systems broadly use proof-size and outcome objectives to guide best-first search. **What would make it fail:** if trace overlap across puzzles is too low, the archive graph is sparse and bridges are mostly illusory. **Cheap validation gate:** on held-out hard states, test whether two-hop or three-hop stitching through the trace archive produces shorter completions than direct rescue search under the same compute budget. **Timing:** medium-term. citeturn27view0turn19view1turn19view2

**Structured diversity over policy space**  
**Core mechanism:** represent diversity explicitly, either by learning a latent-conditioned family of specialised search policies or by imposing repulsion inside the beam based on support signatures, moved-piece patterns, or other path descriptors. This is stronger than symmetry/inverse variants or random seed diversity because it tries to allocate beam budget across genuinely different *search behaviours*. **Why it might beat the current setup:** you already know diversity helps. The question is whether your present diversity sources are mostly cosmetic. Policy-space diversity lets the model express multiple competent search modes rather than one mode with noise. **External evidence:** COMPASS learns a latent space of diverse specialised policies explored at inference time and outperforms standard search strategies on several benchmarks; POMO exploits multiple optima and symmetry to stabilise diverse reinforcement learning; determinantal and diverse beam search explicitly optimise intra-beam diversity; recent population-based NCO work argues that policies operating on sets of candidates can improve robustness and exploration. **What would make it fail:** the diversity descriptor may not correlate with useful frontier separation, so you simply spread budget across weaker modes. **Cheap validation gate:** fix total expansions, vary only the diversity mechanism, and measure best-of-\(K\) path length plus overlap statistics of frontier states or support patterns. **Timing:** medium-term. citeturn25search1turn24search1turn25search3turn25search7turn25search0

**Short-horizon rollout-calibrated reranking**  
**Core mechanism:** for a small subset of frontier candidates, run very short probe searches or rollouts, use the resulting improvement signal as a target, and train a reranker to predict “payoff after \(t\) additional expansions”, not just static remaining distance. In effect, learn which node is most *searchable* under your actual continuation policy. **Why it might beat the current setup:** this directly targets the mismatch between a good heuristic state and a good beam state. Some nodes are not close to solved, but they are easy for the beam to exploit. **External evidence:** Simulation-guided beam search explicitly combines policy scores with rollout information; Limited Rollout Beam Search improves inference and even supports online adaptation; verifier-in-the-loop theorem-proving work uses local look-ahead feedback to improve global search efficiency. **What would make it fail:** probe searches may be too noisy or too expensive relative to the marginal gain. **Cheap validation gate:** compute the correlation between a cheap probe score and eventual giant-beam rescue success on archived frontier nodes; if the probe contains signal beyond current value/Q, this direction is alive. **Timing:** near-term. citeturn6view0turn17view2turn29search12

**Destroy–repair over complete move sequences**  
**Core mechanism:** treat a candidate solution path as the object to optimise. Destroy a segment, support pattern, or macro-block; then repair the gap with a constrained search or learned repair policy conditioned on both boundary states. This is a much larger neighbourhood than local move cancellation and much more principled than generic post-processing. **Why it might beat the current setup:** the literature is strongest when local search is applied over complete candidates, not when one hopes the heuristic will magically find shorter paths from scratch. You already have path rescue/min-merge; this is the next formalisation of that idea. **External evidence:** Neural LNS and dynamic partial removal show that learned destroy–repair operators can outperform handcrafted LNS; the 2026 “LNS meets iterative neural heuristics” paper finds a consistent pattern in which stochastic destroy plus greedy repair is especially effective; NeuRewriter shows that local rewrite policies can iteratively improve combinatorial solutions. **What would make it fail:** if repaired segments mostly recreate the original path or produce legality issues, the neighbourhood is too loose. **Cheap validation gate:** take a bank of current best solutions, destroy only the segments with high model uncertainty or high local branching ambiguity, and see whether repair can shorten paths on a small offline benchmark. **Timing:** medium-term. citeturn31search10turn22view0turn31search8turn23search0

**Elite-set path relinking in path and state space**  
**Core mechanism:** maintain a diverse elite archive of good full solutions or strong partials for each puzzle, then search trajectories connecting them. Use truncated relinking near the ends, mixed forward/backward relinking, or greedy-randomised path relinking instead of one deterministic merge path. **Why it might beat the current setup:** your notes already mention path repair and relinking. The important upgrade from the metaheuristics literature is that relinking works best when there is explicit elite-set management, diversity control, and truncation. It is not just “blend two paths and hope”. **External evidence:** the path-relinking literature shows that relinking intensifies and diversifies search through elite connections; best improvements often occur near the ends of the connecting path; tabu search with path relinking beats pure tabu search on routing problems. **What would make it fail:** if your elite paths are too homogeneous, relinking adds cost but little novelty; if they are too heterogeneous, the intermediate bridge is mostly garbage. **Cheap validation gate:** on puzzles where you already have several distinct near-best paths, compare current relinking/min-merge against truncated end-focused relinking with an explicit elite archive. **Timing:** near-term. citeturn22view1turn22view2

**Adaptive solver portfolio with cold restarts and queue switching**  
**Core mechanism:** treat your existing search modes as a portfolio: forward, inverse, symmetric variants, exploitative beam, diverse beam, rollout-reranked beam, repair-heavy beam, and so on. Train a lightweight selector or scheduler from early-search statistics to decide where the next budget is best spent; include occasional “cold” restarts that deliberately forget bad frontier commitments. **Why it might beat the current setup:** once a stack is already rich, gains often come from allocation rather than invention. SAT work shows that keeping the wrong search information can trap a solver; planning work shows gains from multi-queue search and portfolios; neural solver selection shows that different solvers dominate on different instances. **External evidence:** numeric planning benefits from multi-queue search and portfolios; neural solver-selection work explicitly argues for solver zoos; cold restart strategies in CDCL can outperform warm restarts by periodically discarding harmful search bias. **What would make it fail:** if the portfolio members are too correlated, the selector has nothing meaningful to learn. **Cheap validation gate:** from historic runs, predict the eventual winning variant using only shallow-search features; if the prediction is meaningfully better than random, online budgeting is justified. **Timing:** near-term. citeturn21view1turn21view0turn20search13

**Automatic option discovery from elite traces**  
**Core mechanism:** mine short move subsequences or conjugate-like fragments from elite search traces, but only keep those that have small support, strong inverse/symmetry behaviour, and repeated utility across puzzles. Use them as extra search operators or as an abstract first-stage search vocabulary. **Why it might beat the current setup:** this is not plain commutator-window replacement. It is a data-driven attempt to discover options that collapse depth, create better abstractions, or expose bridge moves invisible to the primitive generators. **External evidence:** classical planning benefits from learned macro-actions and macro-actions with focused effects; sequential-pattern mining has been used to discover useful macros; Santa 2023 discussions and later CayleyPy commentary note that small-support generators can make very simple heuristics useful, while also warning that the generality of the approach is unclear. **What would make it fail:** the option set may explode branching factor, or the discovered patterns may overfit individual puzzles. **Cheap validation gate:** add only a very small option library and ask whether fixed-budget search reaches deeper useful frontiers faster than with primitive moves only. **Timing:** medium-term. citeturn10search17turn10search9turn28search8turn28search20

## Ideas that sound tempting but are probably traps

A bigger backbone, longer training, or another generic Bellman variant is the most obvious trap. Your own empirical summary already points that way, and the external literature mostly supports it: current gains in neural combinatorial optimisation are increasingly coming from search-aware objectives, online adaptation, rollout-guided inference, or self-improving search loops rather than from incremental representation scaling alone. citeturn18view1turn6view0turn17view2turn27view1

Pure RL from scratch on terminal solution length is another trap. Sparse-reward planning problems have been hitting this wall for years: DeepCubeA needed training distributions specifically shaped to propagate reward, and more recent successful work in CO tends to use self-improvement, preferences, local search, or search-generated labels to make the signal dense and low-variance. If RL is used here, it should be of the “preference over search outcomes” or “search-guided hard-case fine-tune” kind, not plain policy gradient over full solve episodes. citeturn5view3turn17view1turn30view0turn27view1

Naive end-to-end differentiable beam search is likely a third trap. Differentiable top-\(k\) operators are elegant, but the literature that actually wins in beam-based settings usually falls back to cheaper surrogates or beam-aware ranking losses, because fully simulating beam dynamics in training is expensive and brittle. That is exactly why BEAR optimises a necessary condition instead of simulating beam search. citeturn7search0turn18view1

A full “build a true optimal Rubik-style abstraction/PDB solver for Megaminx” programme is more of a moonshot than a next move. Pattern databases and abstractions are unquestionably powerful on Rubik’s Cube, but they are memory- and engineering-heavy, and the abstraction literature itself shows that abstraction quality is highly problem-specific. For your leaderboard objective, small auxiliary abstractions might still help as tie-breakers or lower-bound features, but making PDBs the primary bet is unlikely to be the fastest route to sub-70k. citeturn10search2turn28search2turn28search17turn28search6

Finally, “just switch to MCTS” looks tempting because AlphaZero is in the current stack, but the outside literature does not support it as the default answer here. In theorem proving, carefully scaled best-first search has recently challenged the assumption that more elaborate tree search is necessary at all, and Q*/beam-style reorganisations often win by changing queue discipline and supervision rather than by adding Monte Carlo machinery. citeturn19view0turn5view0

## Open questions and limitations

Two uncertainties are worth being explicit about. First, some of the best ideas here are supported by strong evidence in routing, theorem proving, and generic planning, but not yet by public Megaminx-specific ablations. That is especially true for destroy–repair, policy-space diversity, and solver portfolios. I think the transfer is plausible because the search-control problem is structurally similar, but it is still transfer rather than direct proof. citeturn22view0turn25search1turn21view0turn19view0

Second, the most “group-theoretic” direction—option discovery through small-support effects or mined macros—has the largest upside uncertainty. Public CayleyPy/Santa commentary explicitly says the small-support-generator trick was remarkable in other puzzles but that its generality is unclear. So I would treat that as a controlled research branch, not as the mainline plan. citeturn28search8turn28search20

A smaller caveat applies to Q*-style frontier search: the theory is attractive, but much of its empirical edge comes from larger action spaces than Megaminx’s 24 primitive actions. It is still worth testing because of queue discipline and partial expansion, but I would not assume huge gains a priori. citeturn5view0

## Prioritized research agenda

The first thing to investigate is **beam-search-aware supervision**, specifically a retention or cutoff-margin objective over actions on archived giant-beam traces. This is the cleanest expression of your own intuition that the model should predict not just distance, but beam usefulness. It is also near-term, cheap to validate, and strongly supported by both BEAR and beam-policy learning work. citeturn18view1turn8view0turn8view1

The second thing to investigate is **hard-case self-improvement from search outcomes**, using either pairwise preferences or nested-beam labels. I would focus only on the hardest archived puzzles and the states where current search repeatedly drops the winning suffix. This is the most principled way to test whether short 100–500 epoch fine-tunes can help: they can, but only if they are driven by informative search-derived signals rather than more generic value training. citeturn19view0turn27view1turn30view0turn17view1

The third thing to investigate is **solution-level improvement over elite paths**, with two branches evaluated side by side: truncated elite-set path relinking and destroy–repair over full move sequences. I would not implement a grand framework first; I would test whether either branch can shorten an offline archive of already-strong solutions at a non-trivial rate. If that rate is real, this becomes the most plausible route to taking chunks out of the long tail without needing even larger beams. citeturn22view1turn22view2turn31search10turn23search0
