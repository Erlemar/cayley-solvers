## Why the random-walk warm-up exists and where it breaks

The core reason for starting with random walks is not mainly “to learn a good representation”. It is to create a cheap reverse curriculum and dense labels around the solved state, so the network starts with a non-trivial signal instead of a sparse-reward RL problem. The CayleyPy paper explicitly casts this as learning a diffusion-distance proxy: random-walk step counts are far cheaper to generate than Bellman/Q-learning targets, so they are attractive for warm-up; Bellman refinement is added because it aims at the true shortest-path metric, not the diffusion proxy. citeturn21view2turn21view0turn9search0

So your intuition is basically right, but the more exact version is this: random walks are a **cheap approximation stage**, and Bellman refinement is the **metric-correction stage**. Random walks give coverage, solvability, and upper-bounded labels. Bellman refinement tries to repair the fact that diffusion distance and true distance are not the same object, and can even rank states in the wrong order. The CayleyPy paper says this explicitly: diffusion distance is easy to estimate, but it is not monotone in true distance, and larger random-walk datasets can stagnate rather than keep improving. citeturn21view0turn21view2

There are four distinct reasons random walks are not enough by themselves. First, even with infinite random-walk data, you are still learning the wrong target if you care about shortest paths: diffusion distance is a deformation of geodesic distance, not geodesic distance itself. Second, the same state can be reached by many paths of different lengths, so the target is noisy. Third, once walks are long enough to mix, walk length becomes a weaker proxy for distance because step count increasingly reflects the sampling process rather than shortest-path structure. Fourth, in your current setup the search budget goes to 120 steps, while warm-up labels stop at 80, so the heuristic is definitely being used outside its labelled range. citeturn21view0turn9search1 fileciteturn0file2 fileciteturn0file9

There is also an implementation-level point that matters. The CayleyPy paper describes storing first-visit style walk information, but your current `generate_random_walks` utility emits every state at every walk step and leaves duplicate/noisy supervision to downstream training. That makes the practical random-walk labels potentially noisier than the idealised description in the paper. The Bellman clip in your training loop partly compensates for exactly this problem: it enforces a one-step local consistency target, but never lets the refined label exceed the walk-derived upper bound. citeturn21view2turn21view0 fileciteturn0file10 fileciteturn0file11

Non-backtracking helps because, on tree-like regions, non-backtracking walk length tracks graph distance much more closely, and on regular expanders such walks mix faster than simple random walks. That makes them better for coverage and usually better for denoising trivial reversals. But “mix faster” is not automatically “better forever”: faster mixing is good for exploration, yet once you rely on walk length as a label, too much mixing can make the label less distance-like. That is why Bellman refinement remains necessary even when the warm-up walk is improved. citeturn9search1turn21view0turn21view4

## Variations on your random walk

The most promising walk variants are the ones that change **geometry**, **depth distribution**, and **data quality** separately rather than only increasing one scalar like `k_max`.

A better geometric family would be:

- **Moderate-memory non-backtracking**. Your current `n_back=1` only bans the immediate inverse. A sweep such as `n_back ∈ {2,4,8,12}` is much more plausible than jumping straight to very large memory, because it suppresses short local cycles without fully collapsing the effective branching factor. The CayleyPy paper already argues for non-backtracking on theoretical grounds, and your generator structure makes this a natural next ablation. citeturn21view0turn9search1 fileciteturn0file10 fileciteturn0file4

- **Loop-erased or self-repelling walks**. In probability/statistical-mechanics terms, loop-erased random walks remove loops chronologically, while self-repelling walks bias away from frequently visited territory. Both are ways to reduce revisit noise and broaden coverage. I would not expect exact loop erasure to be cheap enough as the default generator for 500k samples per epoch, but approximate loop-erasure or “least-visited-neighbour” penalties are worth testing on the hardest-depth buckets. citeturn18search0turn18search6

- **Generator-balanced walks**. Megaminx moves are not all equivalent in how they interact over short horizons. A simple improvement is to enforce balanced marginal frequencies over faces or inverse pairs, so the dataset is not accidentally dominated by a few local motifs. This is closer to stratified experimental design than to classical RL, and it is cheap to implement. The motivation comes from your puzzle structure itself: 24 generators arranged as inverse pairs over a highly symmetric puzzle. fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file4

A better depth family would be:

- **Depth-mixture sampling instead of one fixed `k_max`**. Fixed `k_max=80` means each epoch has a very rigid depth profile. A mixture such as short/mid/long walks is better aligned with what the network actually needs: sharp local gradients near solved, plus far-state support for search-time ranking. The curriculum literature on guided search repeatedly finds that “difficulty should track current solver ability”, not remain frozen. citeturn26view0turn10search0

- **Adaptive walk length**. Instead of setting `k_max` once, increase or decrease the sampled depth range using solve-rate, heuristic error, or uncertainty. Teacher-student curriculum methods for guided search improved training time by large factors over static bootstrap/random-walk schemes, precisely because they generate states of the right difficulty for the current model rather than blindly pushing depth outward. citeturn26view0

- **Uncertainty-targeted walks**. Use the current model to identify depths or state classes where neighbour ordering is unstable, then oversample those regions. That keeps random walks as the base generator, but makes them active rather than passive. This is conceptually close to bootstrap learning of heuristics and later curriculum-generation work in search. citeturn17search2turn26view0

A better quality family would be:

- **Reverse replay of strong solution paths**. Your code already has a loader for Kociemba-derived walk data: replay a strong solution backwards from the scramble, label each encountered state with remaining path length, and train on that. For Rubik-style puzzles, this produces a much more test-like state distribution than uniform random walks from solved. Even though Megaminx lacks a drop-in Kociemba solver, the same idea can be done with your own beam/MITM solutions. This is one of the strongest low-risk ideas in your current codebase. fileciteturn0file10 fileciteturn0file9

- **Symmetry-canonicalised walks**. If the same essential state appears under rotation, canonicalising it before storage increases effective coverage and reduces data duplication. Your notes already identify Megaminx rotational symmetry as a potentially huge win for search; the same argument applies to training data. fileciteturn0file3

## Alternatives to random walks

The cleanest alternatives are not “more exotic walks”; they are **better state generators**.

The first and most immediately actionable alternative is **exact shell sampling**. Your code already supports sampling exact states from a BFS table, and your notes already emphasise a BFS-d6 shell as high ROI. Exact shallow states are invaluable because they anchor the heuristic with unbiased, low-noise labels near the goal, which is precisely where search most needs accurate local ordering. This is the classic logic behind pattern databases and perimeter search in puzzle solving, and it is still one of the strongest baselines in the field. fileciteturn0file10 fileciteturn0file11 fileciteturn0file3 citeturn9search3

The second alternative is **search-generated curriculum**. Instead of drawing states from unguided random walks, solve batches of training instances with the current solver, then replay those successful trajectories backwards to create new labels. This is the spirit of bootstrap learning and teacher-student curriculum generation for guided search. In that literature, learning interleaves with solving and the next batch of states is chosen to be just beyond the current frontier of competence, which is usually much more sample-efficient than a fixed random-walk generator. citeturn17search2turn26view0

The third alternative is **goal-conditioned hindsight relabelling**. In sparse-reward RL, hindsight experience replay converts failed attempts into useful supervision by relabelling them with goals that were actually reached. DeepCubeAI and related work explicitly combine Q-learning with hindsight replay in a pathfinding setting. For you, the analogue would be to turn partially solved beams into extra supervision: the deepest or best leaf becomes a temporary subgoal, and the path to that leaf becomes exact supervision for a shorter task. That is a principled way to harvest value from failed searches. citeturn22search0turn22search7turn22search13

The fourth alternative is **policy supervision instead of distance supervision**. A recent self-supervised Rubik’s Cube paper trains a network to predict the inverse of the last scramble move, and then searches with cumulative move probabilities rather than a value estimate. On that benchmark it matched or exceeded DeepCubeA while avoiding Bellman-style value learning entirely. I would not assume it transfers cleanly to Megaminx, but it is a real alternative family: learn “what move tends to undo this state” rather than “how far is this state”. citeturn25view0

The fifth alternative is **world-model imagination**. DeepCubeAI-type methods learn a discrete latent world model, then generate training data in that latent space and search with Q*/A*-style algorithms. That is a much larger engineering lift, but it removes your dependence on one particular random-walk generator and generalises across start and goal states. citeturn14search2turn22search7turn15search3

## Variations on Bellman refinement

Your current Bellman stage is a **single-step clipped backup** with a target network. That is a sensible default, but it is not the only Bellman game in town. fileciteturn0file11 fileciteturn0file12

The most important Bellman variants to consider are:

- **Limited-horizon Bellman backups**. Recent work replaces single-step updates with a small search from the sampled state and then backs up from the frontier, which produces a more informative target than `1 + min_a h(s')` while staying cheaper than full exact search. Conceptually, this is a perfect fit for your setting because the expensive part is not applying moves; it is getting a good target from a weak heuristic. citeturn22search11turn22search2

- **Mixed exact and approximate targets**. You already have hooks for exact BFS-d6 states and Kociemba-like paths. Turning those on is not just “more data”; it changes Bellman refinement qualitatively, because now the network is repeatedly tethered to exact near-goal geometry instead of only bootstrapping from itself. In practice this is the lowest-risk Bellman variant available to you today. fileciteturn0file11 fileciteturn0file10 fileciteturn0file9

- **Asymmetric or conservative losses**. In search, the error that hurts most is usually wrong neighbour ordering or dangerous overestimation, not raw MSE. The planning literature now explicitly argues that heuristics should often be trained to rank well rather than estimate cost perfectly. For your model, a loss mixing Bellman regression and pairwise neighbour-ranking would be more aligned with beam search than pure MSE. citeturn17search0turn17search8

- **Approximate-admissibility correction**. There is specific work on turning learned heuristics into approximately admissible ones. For a pipeline like yours, that matters because more conservative heuristics can unlock A*/IDA* variants more safely, or at least reduce pathological over-optimism in beam ranking. citeturn16search0turn16search12

- **Action-value Bellman training**. Instead of learning `V(s)` and then evaluating 24 child states, learn `Q(s,a)` directly. That is still Bellman refinement, just with a different target object, and it lines up naturally with Q* search. This is especially attractive when the action set is modest and you are paying a lot for repeated child evaluations. citeturn17search3turn15search7

- **Distributional or uncertainty-aware Bellman heads**. Rather than a single scalar, predict a small distribution or predictive interval over remaining cost. In rugged search spaces, uncertainty can be used either to diversify beam expansions or to decide where exact-target sampling should be concentrated. This is less battle-tested for twisty puzzles, but it is one of the cleaner ways to separate “the model is wrong” from “the model is unsure”. The recent planning/search literature around learned heuristics is moving in this direction, even when the puzzle-specific literature has not yet standardised it. citeturn15search3turn17search0

## Alternatives to Bellman refinement

If you want to abandon Bellman refinement rather than improve it, there are several real alternatives.

The first is **Autodidactic/approximate value iteration in the DeepCubeA style**. This family starts near solved, expands successors, uses shallow lookahead to generate targets, and iteratively pushes the value function outward. It was a major reason DeepCubeA could solve large puzzles without human data. Relative to your current pipeline, this replaces “warm-up on diffusion labels, then Bellman” with “train on repeated shallow planning targets”. citeturn14search1turn14search0

The second is **direct Q-learning with Q* search**. Instead of a state-value model plus beam search, train a DQN-style action-value model and search with Q*. The point is not only theoretical elegance: Q* was introduced precisely to cut computation when deep networks are expensive and action spaces are non-trivial, because it avoids explicit full child generation for every expansion. citeturn17search3turn15search7turn15search3

The third is **pure policy learning**. The self-supervised inverse-scramble method is one instance; Levin Tree Search and Policy-Guided Heuristic Search are others. In those approaches the learned object is a policy or a joint policy+heuristic, and search is guided by move likelihoods rather than Bellman-consistent values. That can work surprisingly well in puzzles because the inverse path structure is highly regular even when value estimation is noisy. citeturn25view0turn26view0

The fourth is **classical heuristic construction**. Pattern databases, perimeter tables, subgroup chains, and two-phase solvers do not need Bellman learning at all. They need domain engineering and memory, but for puzzles they remain extremely strong because they target admissible or near-admissible structure directly. This is the core lesson from the work of **entity["people","Richard E. Korf","search researcher"]** and **entity["people","Herbert Kociemba","cube algorithm author"]**: in twisty puzzles, search quality often comes from very sharp structural heuristics more than from generic regression. citeturn9search3turn12search1turn12search5turn12search8

The fifth is **group-theoretic function approximation**. Horace Pan and Risi Kondor showed that for permutation puzzles, value functions can be represented in the Fourier basis of the underlying finite group, and that this can outperform standard deep learning baselines on several tasks. That is not a Bellman variant at all; it is a different way to encode the function class you are trying to learn. For Megaminx, it is one of the few “math-heavy” directions that feels genuinely relevant rather than decorative. citeturn20view0

## Alternative end-to-end pipelines

If I had to replace your whole process, I would group the candidates into four tiers.

The highest-confidence replacement is **exact shell + search-generated trajectories + limited-horizon Bellman + MITM/beam**. In practice this is less a total replacement than a re-centering of the pipeline: exact near-goal anchors from BFS-d6, reverse replay from your own strong solves, then stronger multi-step Bellman refinement, then the beam/MITM search infrastructure you already have. This is strongly aligned with your code, your existing notes, and the best ideas from the puzzle-search literature. fileciteturn0file3 fileciteturn0file10 fileciteturn0file11 citeturn22search11turn9search3

The strongest search-centric replacement is **Q-head training + Q* search**. If your wall time is dominated by repeated heuristic evaluations of child states, this can change the economics of search quite a lot. It also pairs naturally with macro-actions, because Q* was designed to remain practical when the action space is enlarged. If you want one “modern DeepCube-style” bet with large upside, this is the one. citeturn17search3turn15search7

The strongest classical-hybrid replacement is **Megaminx subgroup chain / two-phase solving with learned heuristics inside phases**. On Rubik’s Cube, the big leap came from letting structural group theory do most of the global work, then using search intelligently inside the reduced spaces. A Megaminx analogue would not be off-the-shelf, but it is intellectually sound: compress the search space by phase, use exact/perimeter heuristics in early phases, and only use learned heuristics where the subgroup reduction stops carrying you. That is probably the best route if your long-term goal is not merely a Kaggle score but a serious solver. citeturn12search1turn12search5turn12search7turn12search19

The most ambitious representation-level replacement is **group-aware learning**, either via Fourier-basis value functions or via a discrete latent world model. The Fourier route is mathematically closer to the underlying Cayley-graph problem; the world-model route is more ML-standard and may scale better engineering-wise. Both are more speculative than the exact-shell and Q* directions, but both are real alternatives to a flat sticker-permutation MLP trained on walk depths. citeturn20view0turn14search2turn22search7

There is also a genuinely cross-disciplinary, lower-confidence tier: **multi-trajectory search inspired by statistical physics**, such as parallel tempering, population annealing, or sequential Monte Carlo. I would not use these as the main solver, but they are interesting for the hard unsolved tail, because they are specifically designed for rugged landscapes and local minima. In other combinatorial optimisation settings, parallel tempering has beaten simulated annealing, and population annealing has been developed precisely for rough free-energy landscapes. Translated into your setting, that suggests maintaining multiple beams or trajectory populations at different “temperatures” or heuristic sharpness levels, with occasional exchange or resampling. citeturn24search0turn24search8turn24search11turn10search11

First, turn on exact-shell and solver-derived data before replacing the learning algorithm, because those hooks already exist and attack the weakest point of the current generator. Second, move from one-step to limited-horizon Bellman. Third, test a Q-head/Q* branch if profiling still says child evaluation is the main bottleneck. Fourth, if you want a long-horizon research branch, start a Megaminx subgroup-chain or Fourier-basis experiment rather than throwing more ad hoc walk tricks at the same flat MLP. fileciteturn0file9 fileciteturn0file10 fileciteturn0file11 fileciteturn0file3 citeturn22search11turn17search3turn20view0turn12search1
